01 · Reference

Discovery checklist.

Cal runs the qualification call (captured on Fathom). You review the transcript against the checklist below. Anything missing becomes the agenda for your follow-up call with the partner.

How to use

Open the Fathom transcript. Work through each of the eight categories. For each prompt, capture the answer in your own words (free-form is fine, the examples are guidance, not formats). Anything you can't fill from the call is a gap to surface in a follow-up.

Watch for these flags
Weak or missing answers in Strategic context, People, or Commercial are risk signals. Escalate to Cal before proceeding rather than pushing through.

The eight categories

01Strategic context

Understand why this matters to them right now. The "now" answer is more useful than the "what" answer.
Why are they considering social content at this moment in time?
A competitor just gained traction online; a launch or new location; an internal hire pushing for it; investor or partner pressure; a slow quarter and cheap acquisition is needed.
What have they tried before, and what didn't work?
Paid ads with no organic foundation; an in-house attempt that fizzled; a previous agency they didn't click with; nothing yet but they know they should be.
What's their internal capacity for this?
Founder will do it themselves but doesn't know what to film; one staff member is "the social person" but no time; nobody owns it.
What's the urgency or pressure point?
A launch date, trade show, hiring push, competitor move, quarterly review. If there's no urgency, the project will drift.

02Goals

Get past "more followers, more sales" to a specific outcome. The brief is built around the primary goal.
What does success look like 3-6 months after starting?
Tagged by other businesses; founder recognised at industry events; inbound enquiries from social; staff sharing content; hit X followers.
If only one thing changed as a result of working with us, what would it be?
Starting from zero; reviving a dormant account; shifting brand perception from corporate to human; supporting recruitment; supporting paid ads with organic credibility.
How will they know it's working?
Views, follower growth, inbound DMs, attributed sales, internal feedback. Purely vanity metrics are a flag, expectations may need reframing.
Are there any goals their leadership or board cares about that they're being measured on?
MD wants them on TikTok; marketing target tied to organic; benchmarked against a named competitor.

03Audience

Define who the content is actually for. Most brands describe their audience as everyone, you need the real one.
Who is the primary audience for this content?
Existing customers we want to retain; prospective customers in a specific niche; recruits; partners or B2B buyers; investors. Often more than one, rank them.
Where do those people spend time online?
TikTok in the evening; LinkedIn during work hours; Instagram for inspiration; YouTube for long-form. Platforms shape format.
What does this audience respond to from other brands?
Educational; humour; behind-the-scenes; transformation; founder personality. If the prospect doesn't know, it's a research item.
Are there any audiences they explicitly don't want to attract?
B2B brand avoiding consumer enquiries; premium brand avoiding bargain-hunters; recruiter avoiding overqualified candidates.

04Competitive context

Understand the landscape so the brief shows genuine market awareness, not generic research.
Who do they consider their direct competitors?
3-5 named businesses, ideally same niche and geography. Inability to name any is a flag.
Who do they admire, competitor or otherwise?
A national brand they aspire to feel like; a competitor doing social well; a creator in their space. Often the most revealing question.
What do they think competitors do well, and where do they fall short?
X has great production but no personality; Y posts a lot but it's all sales; nobody in our space is on TikTok at all.
Is there a competitor or brand they explicitly don't want to be compared to?
A downmarket version of their offer; an over-corporate competitor; a previous identity they've moved away from.

05Brand context

Understand what's already established so the content fits, rather than fighting the brand.
How would they describe their tone of voice?
Warm and conversational; expert and authoritative; cheeky and irreverent; calm and considered. If they can't articulate one, ask for adjectives.
Do they have brand guidelines, fonts, colours, logos in usable formats?
Full brand book; basic logo pack only; nothing formal but a clear visual style; outdated identity being refreshed.
Are there any topics, language, or imagery to avoid?
Regulated industry restrictions; sensitivities around past issues or competitors; founder doesn't want personal life on camera; off-brand language.
Is the brand in a stable state, or going through change?
Rebrand in progress; new positioning being finalised; product line launching; ownership change.

06People

Confirm who's actually going to appear on camera and how comfortable they'll be. The single biggest variable in shoot-day success.
Who from their team will be on camera?
Founder/MD only; founder plus 2-3 named team members; a confirmed "social champion"; nobody decided yet. The last is a follow-up item.
How comfortable are they on camera currently?
Already posts personally; done video for paid ads; never been filmed; openly nervous. Drives how much coaching the day needs.
Is the founder/MD personally involved or delegating?
Founder will be there and on camera; founder delegating to marketing lead; founder absent from the shoot. Affects authority of content and retainer conversion.
Are there any reluctant participants who've been "voluntold"?
A junior staff member pushed forward by leadership; a department head asked to appear who doesn't want to. Important to know in advance.

07Practical

Confirm the logistics so the day actually happens smoothly. Often skipped on the initial call and becomes a scramble later.
Where will the shoot happen, and is the space suitable?
Their office (is it photogenic?); a workshop or production space; client site; a third location. Dull spaces may need alternatives in the brief.
When are they looking to do this?
ASAP; tied to a launch date; before a quarter-end; flexible. Determines aggressiveness of pre-day timeline.
Are there any access constraints, staff availability, customer presence, equipment, security?
Only weekday mornings; can't film customers without consent; restricted areas; sound-sensitive environment. Shapes the run sheet.
Will they have anything ready for us, products, set dressing, branded items?
Full product range available; samples need ordering; sparse space needs styling; nothing prepared. Affects pre-day asks.

08Commercial

Confirm the deal is real and the next stages are aligned. The "no surprises" stage.
Is the price agreed and is the right person authorising it?
Founder paying personally; marketing budget signed off by FD; pending one more internal conversation. Anything but a clean yes is a risk.
Have they expressed interest in the retainer or scheduling add-on?
Explicitly asked about ongoing; cautious about commitment; only wants a one-off to test; already mentally on a retainer.
Are they likely candidates for cross-sell into other Base Brands services?
Dated website; weak brand identity; paid ads with poor creative; obvious SEO opportunity. Flag but don't pitch, Cal's conversation later.
What's their decision-making style and pace?
Founder-led, fast; committee-led, slower; one strong opinion plus quieter colleagues. Affects how the brief is presented and revision expectations.
02 · Reference

Brief structure.

Every Content Day brief follows the same six-section structure. This page is your reference for what each section contains, what good looks like, and what to avoid. The brief is the most important artefact you produce, it's the "wow, they get us" moment that justifies the engagement and seeds the retainer.

Core principle

Research feeds the brief; the brief drives the day. Every input you gather must serve either the client-facing brief or the internal shoot plan. Anything that doesn't serve one of those two doesn't get done. Stay under 2 hours of research.

The six sections

01

Where you are now

A short, sharp summary of the partner's current social presence and brand positioning, with the audit findings from Fee folded in as evidence. The baseline against which everything else gets measured later.

This section establishes credibility. It tells the partner "we've actually looked at you" before we pitch anything. It also gives both sides a measurable starting point, when they ask "is it working?" in six months, this is what we measure against.

What good looks like
  • 3-5 concrete observations, not generic statements.
  • Real metrics from Fee's audit, followers, posting volume, engagement levels, channel gaps.
  • An honest read of what they're doing well alongside what's missing.
  • Specific enough that a competitor reading it would recognise the brand.
  • Concise, this is context, not the main event. Half a page maximum.
02

Competitor observations

3-5 named competitors with short observations on each, what they do well and what they're missing. The gold here is what they're not doing: the spaces in the market the partner can occupy.

This is where the brief earns its strategic credibility. Generic "your competitors are X, Y, Z" reads like research; specific "here's what Competitor X does that you should consider, and here's what they all miss that you can own" reads like advisory.

What good looks like
  • Real businesses, named, with URLs or handles linked.
  • For each: one strength and one gap. Don't over-explain.
  • A "key opportunity" summary at the end pulling out the white space.
  • Honest, if a competitor is genuinely better at something, say so. Credibility comes from balance.
  • Avoid: vague "they don't have personality" observations. Be specific.
03

Content pillars

3-4 themes that organise everything we'd create together. Each pillar gets a name, a one-sentence definition specific to this partner, and example content types underneath. This is the strategic backbone, every shoot idea ties back to a pillar.

Pillars are the long game. They give the partner a coherent identity over time. They make the retainer easy to sell because pillars compound, month one shows the pillars, month four shows the audience starting to recognise them.

What good looks like
  • 3-4 pillars maximum. More than that and nothing gets reinforced.
  • Each named in 2-3 words ("Family Behind WMR", not "Culture Content"). Memorable beats generic.
  • One-sentence definition that's partner-specific, not industry-generic.
  • Example content types underneath, drawn from the format library plus your own additions.
  • Avoid: pillar names that are just categories. "Educational Content" is a category. "Scrap School" is a pillar.
04

What we'll shoot on the day

10-15 specific shoot concepts mapped to pillars. The 12 that become the content pack get refined from this list, with 2-3 reserves in case something doesn't cut together. Each concept needs a title, a brief description, a pillar tag, a goal, and a filming location note.

This is where the format library does most of its work. You're selecting library entries that fit, adapting them to the specific partner, and adding 2-3 of your own ideas that aren't in the library. The library is a floor, never a ceiling.

What good looks like
  • 10-15 concepts total. More gets diluted; fewer leaves no editing flexibility.
  • Each concept: title, one-line description, pillar, goal, filming location.
  • Goals are per-concept, "Capture personality and chemistry", "Create curiosity and value". This guides Ella's edit later.
  • At least 2-3 ideas that come from you, not the library. Always.
  • Prompt questions for each concept, these set up the on-camera conversation.
  • Avoid: ideas that are too clever to film in 2-4 hours, or that require equipment the iPhone setup doesn't support.
05

Deliverables

What the partner receives. Standard pack is 12 short-form vertical videos plus 2-3 supporting B-roll pieces, with the option to over-deliver if footage allows. Note any add-ons (platform variants, scheduling) and the delivery timeline.

This section is mostly templated, the same shape every time. What changes is the per-shoot detail: which platforms the partner posts to, what the recommended posting cadence is, any specific formats requested.

What good looks like
  • 12 videos as standard. Never promise more in the brief; over-deliver as a surprise.
  • Vertical 9:16, 15-90 seconds, captions burned in, music from curated library.
  • Posting cadence recommendation: usually 2-3 per week, lasting roughly 4-5 weeks.
  • Delivery within 10 working days of shoot.
  • 1 revision round included; reshoots are not included.
  • Add-ons listed clearly with prices if relevant to this partner.
06

What happens next

The soft contract. Confirmation of shoot day, what the partner needs to prep, what to expect afterwards. Functions as the bridge between brief sign-off and pre-day prep. Becomes the "Your Shoot Day" tab on the brief URL once sign-off is complete.

This section closes the loop. The partner should finish reading the brief knowing exactly what happens next, when, and what's expected of them. No "we'll be in touch" vagueness.

What good looks like
  • Shoot date and time confirmed.
  • Who from their team needs to be there, named.
  • A short prep checklist (wardrobe, space, products, anything to have ready).
  • What to expect on the day itself, arrival, walk-through, shooting window, wrap.
  • Post-day timeline, when the content pack arrives, how the review and sign-off works.
  • Avoid: generic "we look forward to working with you" filler. Be specific.

The brief is not

A few things the brief explicitly isn't, because the temptation to scope creep is real:

  • A full marketing strategy. We're recommending content production, not rebuilding their go-to-market.
  • A long-form audit. Fee's audit data goes into Section 1; we don't reproduce it in full.
  • A polished case study. The brief is a working document for the engagement, not a portfolio piece.
  • A pitch deck. The sale happened before the brief. The brief delivers on the sale.
Default discipline
If a section is taking more than 30 minutes to write, the brief is probably trying to do too much. The whole brief should be readable in 10-15 minutes by a non-marketing decision-maker.

04 · Per shoot

Kick-off pack.

Once a partner signs off the strategy, the "Your Shoot Day" tab in their portal unlocks. This is the kick-off pack, six sections of partner-facing prep content plus a personal Loom from you. It's how the client gets ready for the shoot day, and it's how you reduce their anxiety in the 7-14 days before filming.

Where this lives

The kick-off pack is not a separate document. It's the content that populates the "Your Shoot Day" tab in the partner portal after they've signed off the strategy. Same URL, same password, same experience. The portal grows with the engagement.

The tone discipline
This pack must reduce the partner's shoot day anxiety, not create it. Every section is written as reassurance, "here's what to expect", not as instructions. The partner should finish reading and think I'm in safe hands, not I have a lot to do.

The Loom intro.

Before the six sections, a 2-3 minute Loom from you sits at the top of the tab, same placement as the Welcome tab. It's the human moment that anchors the practical content underneath.

What to cover in the Loom:

  • Recap the shoot date and confirm the location.
  • Walk them through what's in the tab so they know what to read.
  • Reassure them about the day, relaxed, conversational, you've done this before.
  • One small ask if there's anything you need them to action (specific person to confirm, prop to gather).
  • Sign off with how to reach you if they have questions.

The six sections.

01

Your shoot day at a glance

The headline summary. Date, time, address, who from Base Brands is coming, how to reach them. Everything the partner needs to remember on the morning of the shoot in one scannable block.

This is the section the partner will check three times in the days before the shoot. Make it impossible to misread.

What good looks like
  • Date and time in clear format ("Thursday, 12 June · 9:30am arrival").
  • Full address with postcode, plus a note on parking and access.
  • Named contact for the shoot (you), with mobile number and email.
  • How long we'll be on site (a window, not an exact time, "2-4 hours").
  • A single sentence summary of what the day will produce ("12 short-form videos plus B-roll").
02

Who needs to be there

The named people from the partner's team, who's expected, what role they're playing on the day, when they need to be available. Surfaces logistical issues early so they get resolved before the morning of the shoot.

The most common shoot-day problem isn't equipment or weather, it's "where's Dave, he was meant to be in shot at 11." This section prevents that.

What good looks like
  • Named individuals with role on the day ("Mark, founder, in most pieces", "Sarah, operations, in 2-3 educational pieces").
  • Time windows when each person needs to be available, not vague "all day".
  • Any conditional notes ("Tom only available before 11am, we'll prioritise his pieces").
  • A polite ask: "If anyone on this list can no longer make it, let us know by [date] so we can rework the shoot list."
  • Avoid: long lists of optional attendees. Confirmed people only.
03

Preparing your space

What to do (and not do) to the physical environment in the days before the shoot. Partner-specific, a scrap yard's prep is different from a hair salon's. Designed to set the visual stage without asking for theatre.

The trick is being specific enough to be useful without being so prescriptive it sounds like a film production demand. Most partners can't reorganise their entire space; we work with what's there.

What good looks like
  • 3-5 specific suggestions, drawn from your knowledge of their site.
  • Frame each as a suggestion not a command ("It'd help if..." not "You need to...").
  • Cover obvious wins: tidy areas we'll film in, branded items visible, anything signature or photogenic surfaced.
  • Note anything to avoid, branded items from other companies, sensitive paperwork, anything they wouldn't want on camera.
  • A reassurance: "Don't over-stage anything, the realness is the point."
04

Preparing yourselves

The human prep. Wardrobe, energy, what to bring, mental approach. The section most likely to reduce on-camera anxiety if written well, most people worry about how they'll look and sound, not the logistics.

Tone is critical here. The goal is permission, not prescription. "You don't need to do anything special" is more reassuring than a list of demands.

What good looks like
  • Wardrobe guidance, what works on camera, what to avoid (fine stripes, busy patterns, logos from other brands). Specific but permissive.
  • A note that they should look like themselves on a normal day, not "dressed up for the camera".
  • Practical: have water nearby, plan ahead so they're not rushing in from another meeting.
  • The "you don't need to memorise anything" reassurance, the prompts make conversation flow, they don't need lines.
  • If anyone's nervous, name it warmly: "First time on camera? Everyone's nervous for the first 10 minutes, it passes quickly."
05

What the day looks like

The hour-by-hour flow of the shoot so there are no surprises. Three phases: arrival and warm-up, core filming, wrap and pickups. Helps the partner understand they're not going to be on camera the entire time, and gives them visibility on their own availability windows.

This section quietly does a lot of work, it frames the day as relaxed and structured rather than chaotic and improvised, which builds trust.

What good looks like
  • Arrival and warm-up (20-30 min), what happens when you arrive, the walk-through, getting people comfortable with the equipment.
  • Core filming (90-120 min), the bulk of the content captured, in roughly the order from the brief.
  • Wrap and pickups (30-45 min), final B-roll, anything that emerged on the day, packing down.
  • Approximate timings throughout, framed as a window not a schedule.
  • What they should and shouldn't do during downtime ("Carry on as normal, we'll grab you when we need you").
06

After the shoot

The handoff to the post-production phase. When the content arrives, how the review works, what happens between now and then. Closes the loop on the engagement and bridges to the "Your Content" tab unlocking.

This section reminds the partner that the shoot isn't the end, it's roughly the halfway point of the engagement, and the next phase has its own structure.

What good looks like
  • Delivery timeline, "Within 10 working days, your content pack will be ready."
  • What they'll receive, link back to the deliverables list from the strategy tab.
  • How the review works, Loom walkthrough from you, 5-day feedback window, 1 revision round.
  • When the "Your Content" tab will unlock and what they'll see when it does.
  • A soft close, "Enjoy the day. The hardest bit is over once we wrap."

Generating the pack.

For now, the kick-off pack is generated by you adapting this structure to each shoot. The structure stays the same; the partner-specific detail changes. The Brief Input Form (when built) will eventually include the fields needed to auto-populate the kick-off pack content into the portal alongside the brief.

Until then: open the partner's portal HTML file, fill in the "Your Shoot Day" tab with the six sections above, upload the Loom intro, and unlock the tab. Should be a 60-90 minute job per shoot.

Default discipline
If the kick-off pack reads like a homework assignment, rewrite it. The whole pack should be readable in 5-8 minutes and leave the partner feeling looked after, not loaded up.
05 · Per shoot

Run sheet.

Your internal shoot day document. Built from the brief, reorganised for the realities of filming, annotated on the day. The run sheet has one job, keep the shoot on track without you needing to think about what comes next.

Where this lives

The run sheet is internal, the partner never sees it. It lives as a Google Doc (or similar) you open on your phone or iPad during the shoot. The Bible section explains the structure; the actual run sheets sit in a separate working folder per partner.

The design discipline
A run sheet that takes more than 15 seconds to find anything in is broken. You're filming, directing, and coaching at the same time, the document has to do its share of the cognitive work, not add to yours.

Generated from the brief.

Most of the run sheet content already exists in the brief, the shoot concepts, the prompts, the filming locations, who's in each piece. You're not writing from scratch. You're copying the brief data into the run sheet template, then adding sequencing and conditional notes.

Expect this to take 30-45 minutes per shoot. If it's taking longer, you're over-engineering.

The six zones.

01

Essentials block

The top of the document. The information you'll glance at on the way to the shoot and again when you arrive. Designed to fit on one phone screen without scrolling.

This is also the section that gets shared with anyone supporting you (Cal, a second shooter, anyone delivering equipment). Treat it as the shareable summary.

What good looks like
  • Date and time in clear format ("Thu 12 June · 9:30am arrival, 1:30pm wrap").
  • Full address with postcode and a parking note ("park in customer area, not yard floor").
  • Primary contact on site with mobile number, usually the founder or named host.
  • Equipment notes, anything beyond standard kit ("bring tripod for the long shot at the scales").
  • One-line shoot goal, the headline ambition for the day ("12 videos across all 4 pillars, lean into family personality").
02

Brief at a glance

A one-screen recap of the strategic context. Not the full brief, the anchoring elements only. Helps you stay aligned with the brief in the moment without re-reading the full document.

This is the section you'll re-read between shots if a piece isn't landing and you need to remember what you were going for.

What good looks like
  • The four pillars listed with one-line definitions each.
  • The headline "what we're trying to achieve" from the strategy.
  • Tone notes, any specific guidance on voice, energy, attitude.
  • Things to avoid, sensitive topics, off-limits areas, language to stay away from.
  • Strictly one screen. If it spills, it's too long.
03

Shot list, in shoot order

The meat of the run sheet. Every shoot concept from the brief, reordered into a sensible day flow. The brief organises content by pillar; the run sheet organises by the realities of filming, location, energy, who's available.

The reordering principles matter. Founders are most energetic in the first hour; sensitive or personal pieces come after warm-up; pieces with multiple people happen when those people are all available; B-roll and wide shots come at the wrap when energy is dropping.

What good looks like
  • Each entry compact, number, title, location, who's in it, key prompts (3-5 max).
  • Estimated time per piece (usually 8-12 minutes per concept including resets).
  • A status indicator you can update on the day, checkbox or simple "done / partial / skipped".
  • Grouped by location where possible, film everything in the workshop in one block, then move outside.
  • Warm-up pieces first (easier, lower stakes), highest-stakes pieces in the middle (peak energy), wrap and B-roll last.
  • Avoid: copying full prompt lists from the brief. Keep prompts to the 3-5 that will actually be used in the moment.
04

Sequencing notes

Guidance on the day's flow that doesn't belong inside individual shot entries. Conditional logic, dependencies, "if X happens, then Y". Small section but high value, these notes prevent the avoidable problems.

This is the section that captures everything you've learned from the discovery call and the kick-off pack about how the day needs to unfold.

What good looks like
  • Time-bound notes ("Tom only available before 11am, prioritise pieces 03 and 07 in the first 90 minutes").
  • Sequencing dependencies ("Clean the yard area before filming piece 05").
  • Energy notes ("Save personal pieces, origin story, family ones, for after first hour").
  • Risk flags ("Mark gets nervous if there's a customer watching, film his pieces in the office, not the yard").
  • Plan-B notes for things that might not work ("If customer reactions don't happen organically, use the prep questions in piece 09 as backup").
05

Just-in-time additions

Empty space at the bottom of the document for things that emerge during the shoot. New ideas, missed shots to come back to, observations to feed back to Ella later. A scratchpad, not a structured section.

The point is that this section exists, not what it looks like. Most of what gets captured here is useful for editing or for the next shoot, keep it loose and quick to add to.

What good looks like
  • A clear empty space, heading "JIT additions" followed by space to add bullet points.
  • Examples already in the template so the pattern is obvious ("e.g. customer Jake said 'best yard in the North West', capture if he's here again").
  • Light annotation only, names, one-line notes, voice memos referenced by timestamp.
  • Avoid: trying to make this section structured. Its value is in being unstructured.
06

Wrap notes

A short post-shoot capture filled in within an hour of leaving the site, usually in the car on the way back. What worked, what didn't, what to remember for the next shoot, what the partner committed to following up on.

These notes feed into two places: the edit brief for Ella (so she knows what the strong pieces are before she opens the footage) and the partner Trello card (so you have a paper trail of what happened on the day).

What good looks like
  • 3-5 bullet points only. Quick capture, not a report.
  • "Strongest moments", the pieces you know will edit well.
  • "Weakest moments", the pieces that might not make the final 12.
  • "For next time", anything you'd do differently or want to remember.
  • "Partner follow-ups", anything they said they'd send you (photos, contacts, missed info).
  • Avoid: writing this the next day. Memory degrades fast. Capture it within an hour.

The relationship to the edit brief.

The run sheet feeds directly into the edit brief Ella receives. The "strongest moments" notes tell her which pieces to prioritise. The "JIT additions" might surface unplanned content worth pulling. The status indicators on each shot tell her what was actually captured versus skipped.

In practice: when you sit down to write the edit brief in the days after the shoot, the run sheet is the document you open first. Everything else builds on it.

Default discipline
The run sheet is a tool, not a deliverable. It serves you on the day. If a section isn't earning its place, if you're never looking at the brief-at-a-glance, if the wrap notes never get filled in, cut it. The template should evolve based on what you actually use.
06 · Per shoot

Edit brief + guidelines.

The handoff to Ella. One document, two layers, the persistent Base Brands editing guidelines plus per-shoot direction for the twelve videos. This is the most consequential artefact in the pipeline, because Ella works from nothing else. Everything we want to see in the final pack has to be in this document.

Where this lives

Internal document. One Google Doc per shoot, generated from a master template that already contains the editing guidelines. You fill in only the per-shoot direction section. Ella never sees the partner portal; this is her primary working artefact.

The single failure mode
Ambiguity. Ella has none of the context you have, she wasn't on the shoot. If she has to guess what we wanted, edits land between fine but generic and wrong. Both cost money. Write to eliminate guessing.

The two layers.

A

Editing guidelines (persistent)

The "house style" reference. Same across every shoot. Ella reads it once when she joins, then references it occasionally. Lives at the top of every edit brief document so it's always available, but in practice Ella will scroll past it after a few projects.

The guidelines section answers Ella's repeat questions in advance, caption style, music library, transitions, pace conventions, what to never do. It's the consistency mechanism.

What good looks like
  • Caption style, exact font, weight, size relative to frame, position, animation in/out, line breaks on natural speech beats.
  • Music library, where to source from (one shared library link), how to choose, any tracks to avoid.
  • Pace conventions, how long the average cut, when to slow down, when to speed up, breath rules.
  • Colour grading, light grade preset, what to adjust per shoot, what never to touch.
  • Transitions, what's allowed (hard cuts, J-cuts, match cuts), what isn't (whip pans, glitch effects, anything that screams "edit").
  • Brand assets, logo placement (if used), end card style, brand colours.
  • What to never do, list out the obvious bad patterns (auto-captions, royalty-free music, jump cuts mid-sentence, drone footage just because).
B

Per-shoot direction (fresh every shoot)

Twelve videos, each with structured direction. This is where the bulk of your effort goes. Each video gets nine fields, written tightly, no flannel.

Format matters. A long list of paragraphs is useless. Structure it as a grid, table, or accordion, one row per video, columns or expandable sections for the fields. Ella should be able to scan all twelve at a glance and drill into one.

What good looks like
  • Twelve videos numbered and titled (matching the brief).
  • For each: hook, arc, pace, what to cut, closing, music direction, caption notes, aspect ratio, sign-off line.
  • Specific to the footage, reference moments you remember, not generic patterns.
  • One row per video, scannable in under 30 seconds, drill-down per video for full detail.
  • Avoid: paragraph-form briefing. Structure the data so Ella can work from it like a recipe.

The nine fields per video.

Each of the twelve videos in a shoot needs these nine fields filled in. Each field is short, a single line or 2-3 lines maximum. Tight and specific beats long and explanatory.

01The hook

First 3 seconds. What stops the scroll.
What grabs the viewer in the opening moment?
A specific line from the footage ("most people don't know this..."), a visual surprise (close-up of cash being counted), a question ("how much do you think this is worth?"), a punchline-first structure.

02The arc

The structural shape of the video.
What's the structure? How does it unfold?
Build to a point ("here's what happens at the scales..."), joke with punchline, reveal ("turns out it's worth £400"), list (three things tradesmen throw away), payoff (problem → solution → outcome).

03The pace

The energy and tempo of the cut.
How does this video feel?
Slow and atmospheric (origin stories, family pieces). Snappy and rhythmic (educational lists, hook-driven content). Mid-tempo with breaths (founder personality pieces). The pace also signals music choice.

04What to cut

Specific moments you noticed that matter.
What from the footage absolutely should make it in?
"The bit at ~minute 4 where Mark laughs and says 'I dunno mate', use it." "Sarah's reaction when the customer arrives." "Wide of the yard at golden hour, around 3:15pm." Specific, with rough timecodes where helpful.
What absolutely shouldn't?
Anything that felt forced. Anything where the partner looked nervous. Anything that revealed information they later asked to keep private. The customer in the green coat we didn't get permission from.

05The closing

How the video ends.
What's the final 2-3 seconds?
Hard cut on a punchline. Slow fade with music. CTA on screen ("link in bio for prices"). Direct address to camera ("come find us"). A button line, the last spoken word that closes the loop.

06Music direction

Genre and feel, not specific tracks.
What does the soundtrack feel like?
"Driving electronic, mid-tempo, no vocals." "Warm acoustic guitar, slow, builds at the end." "Industrial percussion, punchy." "Silence except natural sound." Specific enough to narrow Ella's choices, open enough for her judgement.

07Caption notes

Anything specific to this video's text treatment.
Anything non-standard about captions for this one?
Specific words to emphasise ("most yards DON'T do this"). Lines to drop entirely. Caption hooks that need to land in the first frame ("£12 a kilo. For copper."). Most videos follow the standard caption guidelines, only note variations.

08Aspect ratio

Default is 9:16 vertical. Note exceptions.
What formats are needed for this video?
Standard: 9:16 vertical for Instagram Reels, Facebook Reels, TikTok. Exceptions: if the partner has the Platform Variants add-on, note the additional formats needed (1:1 for Facebook feed, 16:9 for YouTube, 4:5 for Instagram feed).

09Sign-off line

One line that summarises what this video should feel like when it's working.
What's the one-line directive for this piece?
"Make it feel like a friend showing you something cool." "It should make tradesmen text their mate the link." "Sad-funny, Mark is genuinely funny but the topic has weight." A line that captures the *vibe* in a way that resolves arguments about specific edit choices.

The brief is recipe, not essay.

The temptation when writing edit briefs is to explain. To narrate context, justify choices, write paragraphs about what the partner is like. Resist it. Ella doesn't need essays, she needs ingredients and a method.

The structured nine-field format is deliberately recipe-like. Each field has a specific function. Each entry is short. The whole brief should be scannable in 2-3 minutes, drillable when Ella's working on a specific video.

The connection to the run sheet.

When you sit down to write the edit brief, the first document you open is the run sheet, specifically the wrap notes. The "strongest moments" notes shape what to put in "what to cut" for the strongest videos. The "weakest moments" notes flag videos that might need rescuing or replacing. The status indicators tell you what was actually filmed.

If the run sheet is filled in well, the edit brief takes 60-90 minutes. If the run sheet is patchy, you're reconstructing the shoot from memory, which is slow and inaccurate.

Working with Ella.

The first few shoots are a calibration. The format above is the structured ideal, what works on paper. What works in practice depends on Ella's editing process, her tools, her preferences.

Things to watch for in the first 2-3 shoots:

  • Which fields she actually references vs which she skims past.
  • Whether timecodes help or whether rough position is enough.
  • Whether she wants frame-rate, codec, and project setup notes added.
  • Where she's adding her own annotations in the margins, that's the field you're missing.
  • Whether the music direction is specific enough, too vague and she's choosing blind, too specific and you might as well pick the track yourself.

Treat the first WMR shoot as the calibration moment. Update this Bible section based on what worked.

Default discipline
If you find yourself writing more than a few sentences per field, the brief is doing Ella's job for her. Direct, don't dictate. The best edits come from a good editor with a good brief, not a brief so detailed it removes Ella's craft from the equation.
07 · Craft

Filming guide.

The craft fundamentals for iPhone-based content shoots. Principles, patterns, and diagnostics, what good footage looks like, the named scenarios you'll encounter, and what to do when something's going wrong. This guide will evolve as we shoot more, v1 is a starting point, not the final word.

How to use this section

Read it once properly before your first shoot. Reference the patterns section before you arrive at a new location type. Use the diagnostics section in the moment when something feels off. Update this guide based on what you actually learn from each shoot, the visual examples and specific kit notes will get richer over time.

The connecting principle
Every technical decision serves the content. Good light isn't an abstract goal, it's what makes the founder's eyes readable, which is what makes the piece feel intimate. Good audio isn't a box-tick, it's what means viewers don't drop off at second three. Technique serves story, always.

The kit.

Three tiers, what's always in the bag, what you bring when the shoot type calls for it, what's nice-to-have when you have time to plan ahead.

AEssential, always

The kit that goes to every shoot without exception. If any of this is missing or broken, abort.
iPhone (charged, latest model, plenty of storage free)
Minimum 64GB free before the shoot. 4K at 30fps eats roughly 400MB per minute. A 2-hour shoot wants ~50GB free including overhead.
Wireless lavalier microphones
DJI Mic, Rode Wireless GO II, or similar. Two transmitters minimum so two people can be miked at once. Always more than you think you'll need, sound is what people drop off because of.
Handheld gimbal or stabiliser
DJI Osmo Mobile or equivalent. Charged the day before, not the day of. Native iPhone stabilisation is fine for static shots but not for walking-and-talking.
Power bank and charging cables
High-capacity power bank (20,000mAh+). Lightning/USB-C cables for the iPhone, USB-C for the mics. Don't trust the partner's outlets.
Headphones or earbuds
For monitoring audio in noisy environments. The phone speaker won't tell you if there's a buzz or wind issue.

BContextual, bring when needed

Kit you bring when the shoot type calls for it. Decide before you leave based on what you know about the location.
Portable LED light panel
When: dim office spaces, indoor shoots with no natural light, very early or very late in the day. Bi-colour panels are most flexible. Skip for outdoor shoots and well-lit spaces.
Reflector (collapsible, white/silver)
When: outdoor shoots, indoor near big windows, anywhere you need to bounce light back into a face. Cheap, light, transformative. The most under-used piece of kit in iPhone filming.
Mini tripod or phone stand
When: static B-roll shots, locked-off interviews, time-lapses. Anything where you want the camera to stay still while you talk to the person.
Windshields/deadcats for the mics
When: any outdoor shoot, or indoor with strong air conditioning/extractor fans. Wind noise destroys footage and can't be fully fixed in post.
External SSD or transfer hub
When: full-day shoots or back-to-back days. Offload footage during breaks rather than at the end. Reduces risk of losing a day's work to a corrupted phone storage.

CNice to have, pre-plan

Kit that lifts production quality when you've planned ahead. Optional but the difference between OK and great.
Variable ND filter for the phone
When: bright outdoor shoots where the iPhone can't expose properly. Cuts the light without affecting colour. Niche but powerful for outdoor settings.
Spare iPhone (or borrowed second device)
For multi-angle shoots, or as a backup if your primary fails. Most useful for "founder talks to customer" pieces where two simultaneous angles transform the edit.
Clapperboard or sync method
When: multi-device shoots. A clap of hands works fine, anything that produces a visible spike in audio across both devices for sync in the edit.

iPhone settings.

Set these once before your first shoot, then forget about them. The temptation is to fiddle with settings between takes, don't. The settings below are good enough across most scenarios; chasing perfect settings on the day means you miss the moment.

01Resolution & frame rate

The format your footage gets captured in.
Default: 4K at 30fps
Why: high enough resolution to crop and reframe in the edit (important for vertical delivery from horizontal-friendly framing). 30fps reads naturally. Avoid 60fps unless you specifically want slow-motion, the look is different in a way audiences clock.
Exception: 4K at 60fps for action B-roll
Machinery in motion, hands working, anything you might want to slow to 50% in the edit. Don't shoot everything at 60fps, only the specific B-roll moments where slow-mo serves the piece.

02Focus & exposure

Where the iPhone is looking and how it's reading the light.
Tap to focus on the subject's face, then lock
Tap-and-hold on the face until "AE/AF LOCK" appears. This stops the iPhone hunting for focus mid-take when someone moves their hand into the frame. Re-lock between takes if framing changes meaningfully.
Slide exposure down slightly if anything's blowing out
After locking focus, slide the little sun icon down a touch. Easier to recover shadows in the edit than to recover blown highlights. Better to have a slightly darker face than a white wall behind them.

03Stabilisation

Keeping the footage steady.
Native stabilisation is fine for static and slow movement
Hand-held talking-head pieces, slow pans, B-roll captured while standing still. The iPhone's built-in stabilisation handles these well.
Gimbal for any walking, motion, or extended hand-held
Walking and talking, following the founder around the yard, capturing motion that takes more than 10 seconds. Don't try to wing it hand-held, the wobble compounds.

04Audio

What the microphones are doing.
Always use the wireless lav mics, never the phone's built-in mic
The phone mic picks up everything in the room, fans, traffic, your footsteps. The lav mic is positioned 6 inches from someone's mouth and captures their voice cleanly. The single biggest quality difference between amateur and professional content.
Monitor audio with headphones during the first take of each piece
Check for buzz, wind, distortion, or the mic clipping. If anything's off, fix it before the second take. You can't fix bad audio in post, only mask it.
Position the lav mic at chest level, not collar
High enough to capture voice clearly, low enough to avoid clothing rustle. Clip to a shirt button or a discreet point. For high-necked tops, ask the subject if you can clip to their shirt rather than working around it.

The named scenarios.

Common location types you'll encounter, with the pattern for each. Read these before arriving at a similar setting, the right preparation makes the technical decisions a 30-second job on the day instead of a 30-minute one.

01

Workshop or industrial space

Examples: scrap yard, manufacturing floor, mechanic, brewery. Often mixed lighting (overhead fluorescents, daylight from windows, shadows from machinery). Loud with intermittent industrial noise. Lots of visual texture but unpredictable composition.

The pattern
  • Find the spot where natural light from a window or doorway hits the subject's face, favour this over overhead fluorescents.
  • Position the subject so background includes industrial texture (machines, materials, scale) but isn't busy enough to distract.
  • Pause filming during loud intermittent noise, extractor fans cycling, machinery running. Don't try to talk over it.
  • Bring wind protection for the mics, air movement from extraction systems is more constant than you'd think.
  • Embrace the mess. Workshop spaces look most authentic when they look used. Don't ask the partner to tidy beyond removing genuine hazards.
02

Outdoor, harsh sun

Mid-day in summer, bright overcast that's actually sharp, direct sun anywhere. The hardest scenario for iPhone filming because the dynamic range exceeds what the sensor can handle.

The pattern
  • Avoid putting subjects in direct sun if possible, find open shade (under a tree, in a doorway, beside a wall).
  • If direct sun is unavoidable, position the subject so the sun is *behind* you (the cameraperson), not behind them. Backlight equals silhouette.
  • Use a reflector to bounce light back into the face if the subject is in shade with bright background.
  • Slide exposure down, let the background blow out a bit rather than the face go dark.
  • Watch for sweat. Have water nearby and don't push through if the subject is visibly uncomfortable, it shows on camera.
03

Outdoor, overcast

Soft cloudy day, golden hour, anything where the sky is acting as a giant softbox. The easiest outdoor lighting condition and often the most flattering.

The pattern
  • Position the subject so they're facing the brightest part of the sky, usually toward the horizon, not directly up.
  • Reflector still helps lift shadows under the eyes, especially in deep overcast.
  • Watch for wind, overcast days are often windier. Mic protection matters.
  • Embrace the lower contrast, the look is naturally cinematic. Don't try to push colour or contrast in the edit.
  • Golden hour (the hour before sunset) is genuinely magic. Plan high-stakes pieces here when possible.
04

Dim office or windowless room

Standard office with overhead fluorescents, basement spaces, anywhere indoors without natural light. The iPhone struggles in low light, producing noise and washed-out colour.

The pattern
  • Bring the LED light panel, this is exactly what it's for.
  • Position the light at roughly 45 degrees from the subject, at face height, with the panel diffused (most have built-in diffusion).
  • Avoid filming under overhead fluorescents alone, they produce a green cast and hard shadows under the eyes.
  • If there's any window light at all, prioritise it and supplement with the LED panel rather than replacing.
  • White balance can drift indoors. If colours look off in playback, manually set white balance to match the dominant light source.
05

Kitchen, café, or hospitality space

Mixed lighting (warm overheads, daylight from windows, ambient candles). Often visually rich with products, but acoustically busy with extraction, espresso machines, customers.

The pattern
  • Film during the quietest slot of the day, usually mid-afternoon between lunch and dinner service.
  • Position the subject near a window where possible, kitchens look beautiful when the daylight competes with the warm tungsten.
  • Mic protection is essential, extraction fans run constantly in commercial kitchens.
  • Capture detail B-roll (hands plating, ingredients, espresso pouring) at the same time as conversation, the visual richness is the value.
  • Get explicit permission to film any customers in the background, never assume.
06

Customer-facing area

Reception, retail floor, waiting room, anywhere customers might appear. Common for "customer reactions" or "real moments" pieces. Variable conditions, ethical considerations around consent.

The pattern
  • Verbal permission from any identifiable customer before they're in shot, and again before footage is used. Document it.
  • Default to wide enough framing that customers can be in the environment without being the focus.
  • If a specific customer becomes the subject of a piece (e.g. reaction at the scales), get explicit permission, ideally written.
  • Be ready to pause filming if a customer asks not to be, never argue.
  • Be respectful, the customer is there for the business, not for us. Their experience shouldn't suffer because we're filming.

When it's going wrong.

The diagnostics section. Quick fixes for the most common problems you'll hit. Reference this in the moment.

!The subject is silhouetted

Their face is dark; you can see them as a shape but not read their expression.
The cause
Light source is behind them, window, doorway, bright sky. The iPhone is exposing for the bright background, leaving the face underexposed.
The fix
Move the subject so the light is behind you, not them. Or tap their face to re-expose for the face (will blow out the background but face is what matters). Or bring out the reflector and bounce light back into the face from your side.

!The audio is buzzing or hissing

A consistent low-level noise underneath the dialogue.
The cause
Usually mic gain set too high. Sometimes interference from electrical equipment (extraction fans, fluorescent ballasts, dimmer switches).
The fix
Lower the mic gain on the transmitter. Move away from sources of electrical interference. For persistent buzz with no obvious source, try changing the mic's transmission channel, sometimes nearby devices share frequency space.

!The footage looks jittery

Slight micro-shakes that make the video feel uneasy to watch.
The cause
Hand-held filming during walking or movement, with iPhone stabilisation working but not enough. Often noticeable on playback even when the shot felt steady.
The fix
Switch to the gimbal for any moving shots. For brief static pieces, brace your elbows against your body and breathe slowly. The gimbal is the answer 95% of the time, if you're hand-holding moving shots, you're working too hard.

!Colours look washed out or wrong

The image lacks saturation or has a colour cast (green from fluorescents, orange from tungsten, blue from cloudy daylight).
The cause
Auto white balance is averaging mixed light sources, or the dominant light source is producing a colour cast the iPhone hasn't compensated for.
The fix
Manually set white balance for the dominant light. Eliminate competing light sources where possible (turn off the overhead fluorescents, work with window light only). Mild casts can be fixed in the edit; severe ones can't.

!The subject is reading from a script or looking nervous

Their delivery feels stiff or rehearsed; eyes are flicking off-camera.
The cause
They're trying to remember a line, or they've decided what they want to say and are working hard to deliver it. Performance, not conversation.
The fix
Stop filming. Have a real conversation off-camera for 30-60 seconds. Ask them an unrelated question. Get them to laugh. Then restart, but instead of asking the question on the run sheet, ask the conversation back. "Tell me what you just told me again, that bit about the customer with the bag of copper."

File management & backups.

The least glamorous part of the day and the most important. Lost footage is unrecoverable; everything else can be fixed in post.

  • Before the shoot: confirm iPhone has 50GB+ free storage. Charge the iPhone, the gimbal, the mics, the power bank.
  • On the day: don't delete anything mid-shoot. Storage panic leads to lost takes.
  • Within an hour of wrap: offload all footage from the iPhone to an external SSD or laptop. Don't rely on the phone alone.
  • Same day: upload the day's footage to cloud storage (Google Drive, Dropbox, or whatever the agency standardises on). One local copy + one cloud copy = two-place redundancy.
  • Within a week: don't delete from the iPhone until you've confirmed both backups are complete and the edit brief is being worked on.
  • File naming: simple and consistent. "[Partner]_[Date]_[ClipNumber]" or similar. Avoids confusion when working across multiple shoots.
The shoot-day mindset
You are filming, directing, and coaching simultaneously. The technical layer needs to be boring, settings that work, kit that's reliable, patterns you've rehearsed. The interesting part is what happens between you and the person on camera. Technical decisions are the platform, not the performance.

What this guide will become.

This is v1, written from general best-practice with no shoot-specific examples yet. As we shoot more, this guide grows in two ways:

  • Visual examples. Annotated screenshots, side-by-side good/bad shots, kit photos, lighting diagrams. The single biggest lift to this section comes from real photographs taken on real shoots.
  • Specific patterns. Named scenarios will be replaced or supplemented with patterns from actual locations ("WMR scrap yard", "AG Osteo treatment room", "Boogie Bugs dance studio"). The principles are timeless; the patterns get more specific over time.

After every shoot, spend 10 minutes capturing anything you learned that's worth adding here. Lessons from one shoot become principles for the next.

08 · Craft

Banter prompts.

The universal personality-extracting questions that work across formats. The kind you used on the WMR brief, "who's the biggest moaner in the yard?", "who's the boss really?". They're not tied to specific shoot concepts; they're a category of question that pulls personality out of any subject. Reach for them when conversation stalls, when the founder is being too rehearsed, or when you want to lift the energy in the room.

How to use this section

Read it once to internalise the pattern, then reference it sparingly. The starter bank below isn't a script to read from, it's ammunition for moments when your own observation isn't enough yet. Most good prompts will come from things you notice on the day. The bank is your safety net.

The honest truth about prompts
Prompts read off a list kill the magic. They land when they emerge from observation, you noticed someone roll their eyes, you ask about it. The bank below is training data for your instinct, not lines to deliver. The goal is to not need this section after the third shoot.

The five things that make a prompt work.

These are the characteristics shared by every prompt that's ever pulled a great moment out of someone on camera. Internalise the pattern and you can generate your own prompts on the fly.

01Specific, not generic

Targeted at a person, moment, or situation, not open-ended.
Why it works
"What's something you do every day that nobody notices?" pulls real answers. "Tell us about yourself" pulls rehearsed corporate language. Specificity gives the subject permission to be specific back.

02Assumes something true

Presupposes a fact and asks for the reaction, not the fact itself.
Why it works
"Who's the biggest moaner?" assumes someone is. The subject reacts to a claim, much easier for nervous people than constructing an answer from a blank page. Compare to "Is there any banter in the team?", flat and direct.

03Slightly mischievous

Invites gentle gossip, mild self-deprecation, or affectionate teasing.
Why it works
The mischief is what creates the "did she just say that?" moment. It signals the conversation is real, not corporate. The subject feels invited to be honest because the asker has set the tone.

04Work as second or third questions

Land best after warmth is established, usually 10-15 minutes in.
Why it works
Opening with "who's the biggest moaner?" feels invasive. Asking it 15 minutes in feels like banter between people who already trust each other. The earlier conversation earns the right to ask.

05Invite stories, not answers

The best prompts produce anecdotes, not yes/no responses.
Why it works
"I'll tell you who, it was Dave last Tuesday, you should have seen him..." is content. "Yes" is not. If a prompt produces flat answers, it's not doing its job, adjust or drop it.

The starter bank.

Six categories of prompts, ordered roughly by sequence in a shoot, start light, build into deeper territory as warmth develops. Use these as fuel, not as a script. The best moments come from your own observation; these are for the gaps.

01

The team

Low-stakes opener. Who's who, who does what, who has what reputation. Establishes the cast of characters and gives you names to refer back to throughout the shoot.

Starter prompts
  • Who's always first in?
  • Who does the least work round here?
  • Who thinks they own the place?
  • Who's the biggest moaner?
  • Who's the biggest brew-monster?
  • If [name] called in sick tomorrow, what would actually fall apart?
  • Who's been here the longest?
  • Who's the newbie everyone's still pretending to remember the name of?
  • Who do customers always ask for?
  • Who's the secret expert on something nobody knows about?
02

The daily routine

How the day actually flows. The unwritten rules, the small rituals, the things that only happen because someone insists on them. Brings out culture without naming it as culture.

Starter prompts
  • What happens in the first 30 minutes every day?
  • What's the most unnecessary thing you do every morning?
  • How many brews do you get through a day?
  • Who disappears for an hour every day, and where do they go?
  • What's the unofficial lunch rule?
  • What's the last thing that happens before everyone goes home?
  • What's the one thing people are always running out of?
  • What's the radio always tuned to, and who decides?
03

The customers

Stories from the front line. Who comes in, what they ask for, who's memorable. Hugely valuable because customer anecdotes are inherently watchable and they pre-qualify future customers.

Starter prompts
  • What's the weirdest thing someone's brought in / asked for?
  • Who's the customer you'll never forget?
  • What's the most surprising thing customers tell you?
  • What do customers think they want vs what they actually need?
  • What's the most awkward conversation you've had with a customer?
  • Who travels the furthest to come here?
  • What's the question you get asked five times a day?
  • What do new customers always misunderstand?
  • What's the best feedback you've ever had?
04

The work itself

What's weird, hard, or surprising about the actual job. The bits outsiders don't see. Pulls out expertise positioning without it sounding like a CV.

Starter prompts
  • What's the hardest part of the job that nobody talks about?
  • What's the boring bit that's actually the most important?
  • What surprised you about this job in your first year?
  • What do you do that you'd never tell a customer because it'd worry them?
  • What's the bit you secretly love?
  • What's the bit you'd happily never do again?
  • What's the smallest detail that makes the biggest difference?
  • What's the mistake every newbie makes?
05

The industry

What other people in the trade get wrong. Strong opinions about peers. Where the industry's heading. These prompts pull out the founder-voice content that builds authority.

Starter prompts
  • What's the biggest myth about your industry?
  • What do other [trade] do that drives you mad?
  • What's something everyone in your industry agrees on that's actually wrong?
  • What's changed most in the last 10 years?
  • What would you do if you were starting from scratch tomorrow?
  • What's the question journalists always ask that misses the point?
  • What do customers think you do vs what you actually do?
  • What's the conversation your industry needs to have but isn't?
06

The personal

Off-camera life. Who they are when they're not at work. The most personal category and the one that lands strongest emotionally, but only after the previous five have warmed the room. Save these for late in the day.

Starter prompts
  • What did you think you'd be doing at 18?
  • Who's the person who'd be most surprised you're doing this now?
  • What do you do when you're not here?
  • What's the bit of this job your family don't get?
  • What's the proudest moment in the business so far?
  • What do you wish you'd known five years ago?
  • If you weren't doing this, what would you be doing?
  • What's the thing you almost gave up on?

Reading the room.

The prompts aren't a checklist to work through. They're an inventory to draw from when you need them. Some shoots use almost none of these because the conversation flows naturally. Other shoots lean heavily on them because the subject is nervous or quiet.

When to lean in

  • Energy is flat and you need to lift the room.
  • The subject is being too rehearsed or formal.
  • You've established warmth and want to move into something stickier.
  • A pause in the brief gives you 10 minutes that could otherwise be wasted.
  • The conversation has produced flat material so far and you need to shake it.

When to back off

  • The subject is genuinely upset, frustrated, or off-balance, wait for them to settle.
  • You're early in the shoot and warmth hasn't been earned.
  • Someone else from their team is in the room and would be uncomfortable being teased about.
  • The previous answer felt forced or performative, the subject isn't ready for the next layer.
  • You can feel yourself reaching for the prompt because you're losing the room, fix the room, don't paper over it with banter.

Building your own bank.

The list above is a starter. The most valuable prompts will be the ones you discover on actual shoots, the questions that turned out to work for a specific subject or industry, that you couldn't have invented in advance.

After every shoot, capture the prompts that worked best. Add them here. Over time, this section becomes a personal toolkit of techniques tested in real conversations, not a generic library. The first WMR shoot is where this starts.

The deepest discipline
The best banter on camera doesn't sound like banter. It sounds like a real conversation that happens to be filmed. If you ever catch yourself performing the banter, asking a question with the camera in mind, you've already lost it. Curiosity is what makes the prompts work. The prompts are training; curiosity is the real skill.
